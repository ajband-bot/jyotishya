2. Remedial Guidance — Dāna & Pūjā

Each remedy below is offered as a supportive practice aligned with this chart's structure — not a guarantee of a specific outcome, and not a substitute for practical action in the corresponding life area.

For Mars (This Lagna's Most Benefic Planet, Exalted)

Dāna: Donating red lentils, jaggery, or red cloth on Tuesdays is the classical charitable practice associated with Mars.

Pūjā: Given Mars's role as 9th-lord-from-both-Lagna-and-Kārakāṁśa (Part 3 §8), a Tuesday visit to a Hanumān temple, or a brief home lamp-offering to Hanumān, is a fitting, well-aligned practice.

Practice: Since Mars is exalted and this Lagna's designated most-benefic planet, channeling its energy into structured physical activity, disciplined courage, and direct (not impulsive) action is itself a form of remedy.

For Jupiter (Lagna Lord & 10th Lord, Doubly Neechābhaṅga-Rescued)

Dāna: Donating yellow items (turmeric, chana dal, yellow cloth) or books to students and teachers, especially on Thursdays.

Pūjā: A brief Thursday evening lamp-offering at a home shrine, or a visit to a temple associated with Jupiter/Guru, is a fitting practice given Jupiter's importance as Lagna+career lord.

Practice: Supporting your own ongoing education, mentoring others, or contributing to a teacher's work all directly and practically strengthen this placement's themes.

For the Ātmakāraka's Gāṇḍānta (Mercury)

Dāna: Donating green items (moong dal, green cloth) or supporting education-related causes on Wednesdays.

Pūjā: A brief Wednesday devotional practice — a lit lamp, quiet reflection — is a fitting complement given Mercury's role as Ātmakāraka.

Practice: Given the retrograde and combust qualities, and the finding that this planet receives zero supporting aspects (Part 1 §5), patience with your own communication and decision-making process — rather than self-criticism when a lesson needs revisiting — is itself a meaningful form of remedy.

For the Sun / Pitṛ Doṣa (Father-Line, Aspect-Based, Moderate Confidence)

Dāna: Feeding crows and cows regularly, and donating food or resources in your father's name (whether living or passed), are both classically supportive practices.

Pūjā: Tarpaṇa (ancestral water-offering rites) during Pitṛ Pakṣa each year is the single most direct classical practice for this placement. Offering water to the rising Sun each morning is a gentle, accessible daily practice that honors this same axis.

For the Moon-Rāhu Conjunction — Grounding the Mind (H6, the chart's most contested house)

Dāna: Donating milk, rice, or white items on Mondays is a traditional practice supporting the Moon directly.

Pūjā: A brief Monday evening lamp-offering, especially during the full moon, is a fitting practice.

Practice: Meditation, time in nature, and consistent sleep/routine discipline are especially valuable given this placement — worth treating as genuine remedial practice, not mere lifestyle advice.

What to approach carefully: Venus and Saturn are functionally compromised placements for this Lagna (Part 1 §8) — amplifying either through gemstones is not recommended by this practice without full consultation, despite Saturn's essential structural role in the chart's central yoga. Structural importance and safe-to-amplify are two different questions.

3. A Suggested Devotional Anchor

Part 3 §8 identified Hanumān as a primary Iṣṭa Devatā candidate, with reasonable confidence given convergence across the two most classically-weighted methods (9th lord from both Lagna and Kārakāṁśa, both pointing to exalted Mars). This is a fitting devotional anchor given the chart's overall signature — Hanumān is, in the whole of Hindu tradition, the deity most associated with forgotten strength recovered exactly when circumstances call for it, echoing the Ahirbudhnya theme of the Lagna Nakṣatra. A Tuesday temple visit, or simply a few minutes of quiet devotion at a home shrine, is a fitting, accessible practice.

The Janma Nakṣatra deity, Bhaga, stands as a warm secondary devatā for matters of relational contentment and prosperity — simple daily gratitude practice is a fitting way to honor this thread. And, in connection with the Ātmakāraka's own nakṣatra deity Indra, a periodic quiet reflection on the responsible use of voice, discernment, and any position of authority held is a subtle but meaningful practice unique to this chart.

Per this document's remedy scope (see header note), no mantra-repetition counts are prescribed here — devotional practice is offered through pūjā, temple visits, and quiet reflection instead.
